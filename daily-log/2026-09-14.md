## 💡 Key takeaways (in my own words)

1. **Context isolation is the strongest argument for multi-agent** — a worker reads 200 documents and returns a page of findings, keeping the lead's window clean. It beats "parallelism" because parallelism alone can be had more cheaply with a workflow. Distinct tool sets (least privilege) and genuine breadth are the other two reasons.

2. **Costs to name cold:** tokens multiply; merging becomes a failure point; debugging gets hard; latency tracks the slowest worker; guardrails must be enforced in N loops instead of one.

3. **A merge strategy has two parts, plus a rule about contradictions.** Workers return *structured findings* (claim, source, date, confidence) into a shared schema — never loose prose. The lead dedupes and applies a stated conflict rule (recency, then source reliability) — and **reports contradictions rather than silently picking a winner**. "The lead figures it out" is not a strategy.

4. **In the drill, multi-agent appears in exactly one stage of six** — *gather*, where the path depends on what the workers find. *Analyse risk* is parallelization because the risk categories are known up front (Day 5's distinction). *Synthesise* stays a single agent because a brief needs one voice. *Resolve the entity* and *validate* are code. A design that makes every stage an agent loses most of the marks.

5. **Failure asymmetry drove the design again** (Day 2). A missed red flag can cost millions; a false one costs an analyst an hour → favour catching every real risk, mandatory human gate, and treat a later-discovered missed flag as the single most valuable eval case.
